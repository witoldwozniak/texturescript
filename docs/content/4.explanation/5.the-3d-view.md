A crop sprite is never seen flat in the game. It is drawn on four vertical planes in a hash
pattern (or two diagonal planes for flowers), lit, cut out at 50% alpha and seen from above and
at an angle. A sprite that looks right flat can read as a smear on the model, and a sparse one
can look full. So TextureScript judges crops in 3D.

## What it draws

`view` and `shoot` render a scene with [deepslate](https://github.com/misode/deepslate), using
the game's own block models read from your jar: the crop and cross models, the game's
directional face shading and its alpha cutout. Your `.stages` files are compiled in memory and
pushed to the page, so an edit to a grid shows up on the next save.

It does not draw biome tint or smooth lighting. Check the final art in the game.

## Why vanilla is next to it

Judging in isolation drifts. A crop that looks fine alone may be twice as dense as wheat, or a
different green from every other plant in the world. Planting vanilla wheat or a sunflower next
to your crop gives a scale and a reference for colour, density and silhouette. The vanilla
blocks come from your own jar at run time and are never written to disk.

## Why screenshots

`view` is for a person at a screen. `shoot` renders the same scene in a headless browser, from
several angles and variants, into one sheet. That makes the 3D judgement available to an agent,
which can look at the image, and to a reviewer, who can see before and after side by side. One
angle hides overlaps, so a sheet usually has two or three.
