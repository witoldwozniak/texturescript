Vanilla Minecraft textures follow conventions nobody wrote down: crops have no outline, items
do; no texture dithers at 16 px; a mature crop covers between a quarter and a half of its
sprite. TextureScript's rules were measured on a selection of vanilla crop and item textures,
not assumed. The authoring skill's `rules/crop.md` and `rules/item.md` record the measurements.

## Thresholds describe vanilla, not taste

Each lint threshold sits at the edge of what vanilla does. The vanilla median is 6 colours and
the maximum 13, so 13 is where `budget` fails. No vanilla crop has more than 12 disconnected
pieces, so 12 is where `islands` warns. Nothing in the rules says what looks good; they say what
looks like Minecraft.

That is why there are two levels:

- **FAIL** means the texture breaks something vanilla never breaks and the game depends on, like
  semi-transparent pixels or a two-block plant whose halves do not join.
- **WARN** means the texture does something vanilla rarely does. It may still be the right call.
  Look, decide, and if a rule is wrong for your texture, say so instead of nudging pixels until
  it is quiet.

Some rules are known to be weak. `light` trips on 5 of 19 vanilla items, so it is a prompt to
look, nothing more.

## Crops and items are different

Crops are drawn on crossing planes and seen through each other, so they are sparse and have no
outline: an outline would draw dark lines through the middle of the plant. Items are flat and
seen against the inventory background, so they carry a one-pixel outline in their darkest shade
and are lit from the top left. `lint --kind` switches between the two sets of rules.

## Density is judged on the model

Measured in game, a crop sprite at 30% coverage looks as full as one at 43%, because the planes
overlap. A flat sprite that looks thin is often right. This is why the coverage rule's range is
wide, and why crops should be judged in `view` or `shoot`, not as a flat PNG.
