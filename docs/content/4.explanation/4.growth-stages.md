A vanilla crop has four to eight growth stages. Drawing each one separately is slow, and
separately drawn stages drift: the stalk moves a column, a leaf attaches somewhere new, and the
plant seems to jump as it grows. TextureScript derives the stages instead.

## Draw the mature plant, then say when each part appears

You draw the mature plant once. Then a **stage map**, a 16×16 grid of digits, labels every
opaque pixel with the first stage it appears in. Stage *n* is the mature plant with only the
pixels labelled *n* or lower.

This captures how vanilla crops grow. Measured on wheat, the pitcher crop and the root crops,
growth is additive in silhouette: the stalk keeps its position, leaves keep their attachment
points, and new parts arrive in later stages. The map makes that the default, so stages cannot
drift.

## Additive in silhouette, not in colour

Vanilla young stages are not just cropped copies of the mature plant. Young wheat is brighter
and greener; ripening recolours most retained pixels. So a stages file adds two transformations
on top of the map:

- **`shift`** draws a stage some shades lighter within each material. Because shades come from
  a ramp, "two shades lighter" is well defined.
- **`recolour`** draws one key as another up to a given stage, for a part that is a different
  material when young, like a root that reads as leaf until it swells.

## When a map is not enough

Some 10 to 15% of a vanilla stage's pixels move in the next stage: a leaf tip re-angles, a
sprout is drawn lower than any mature pixel. A map cannot move pixels. For those stages you edit
the generated grid by hand and regenerate with `--keep`, which keeps your drawing and still
lints it. The generated stages do most of the work; hand edits handle the exceptions.

## Every stage is checked

`stages` lints every half of every stage, checks the seam between the halves of two-block
stages, and refuses a map that does not label exactly the mature pixels. A growth design is
either complete and consistent or it is refused.
